Albert O Hirschman [wrote in 1970](https://www.amazon.in/Exit-Voice-Loyalty-Responses-Organizations/dp/0674276604) that people make either of two choices when they observe decline in a group. These choices are exit (leave the group) or voice (express discontent, raise opinions, propose solutions and persuade other members/leaders). This dissatisfaction could be because of deterioration in any type of group - firms, organisations, or nation-states. 

A customer might move their business to a competing brand (exit) or write to the management/customer service about the declining/poor quality of the product (voice). An employee might express dissatisfaction (voice) through feedback in employee surveys, convey to immediate management, raise questions in townhalls, or join/organise a union protest. Or, they might quit the firm (exit). Similarly, a citizen may raise voice through protests, voting-out the elected representative or just move (exit) to a different city/state/country (also called “voting with one’s feet”).

Voice has more signal than exit. It takes a perceptive group to ascertain that people are exiting because of dissatisfaction and then to determine the cause of dissatisfaction and decline. On the other hand, people generally express their exact concerns while voicing their opinions. The cost-benefit ratio of exit vs voice depends on the type of group. 

Loyalty is a feeling of belonging to the group. A “patriotic” citizen who may feel invested in their country’s progress, might find it difficult to exit/emigrate out even though a country might not be advancing in their opinion. Thus, “loyal” people in a state-citizen relationship will be motivated towards using their voice to reform the system. Further, if the environment for exit/emigration is difficult, it may further tilt the balance towards voice. 

### Urbanisation and retreat of the elite     

Like many developing countries, India is [rapidly urbanising](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/opinion/2024/01/30/gearing-up-for-india-s-rapid-urban-transformation). Cities provide employment opportunities and higher wages, making people move from rural areas to urban. Large number of people working close-by lead to agglomeration effects making cities highly productive and efficient. However, if urban governance does not keep pace with the growing number of people, we start seeing the negative effects of migration into the cities - such as congestion, pollution, and diseases. The decline in living conditions due to these effects, thus, poses the familiar exit vs voice question to the resident. 

While people moving out of India for education or better job prospects is comparatively easily noticeable, there is a second kind of exit that is taking place amongst the Indian elites. This is the elites moving to access-controlled communities within the city. These gated communities privatise many of the local government’s functions - such as providing for public spaces like parks/grounds, well-paved and non-potholed roads, walkable footpaths, waste collection and disposal, water supply (collectivized and managed through RWAs) and sewerage, pest control, and private security among others. Other amenities include swimming pools, sport facilities like tennis courts, private schools and hospitals. Essentially, these societies are filling in for the gaps of the local government, at disproportionate prices, to give the elite experiences of living in a “developed country.” 

This a not a story of villianisation of the elites but of failure of the Indian cities. Elite people, like all people, respond to incentives. In fact, there is a case to be made, that, to an extent, such communities perform the function of standard-setting for the Indian people.  

### Is it all downhill or is there a reason for hope?

We are probably reaching the limits of exiting the Indian city within the city. In general, greater the availability of exit, the less likely it is that voice will be used. In fact, an easier exit functions as a “safety-valve” for rulers as it curtails voicing of discontent, thus maintaining the legitimacy of the regime. However, here is why we are near the limits. 

*First*, the air quality in most major Indian cities has deteriorated to an unconscionably poor level. India [now accounts for 13](https://www.ndtv.com/india-news/worlds-20-most-polluted-cities-13-of-worlds-20-most-polluted-cities-in-india-delhi-most-polluted-capital-7895690) of the world’s top 20 most polluted cities. (Un)fortunately air does not respect community boundaries, forcing everyone to breathe the same toxic fumes. While air purifiers in closed-room settings do help alleviate some of this trouble, it is not practically possible for most people to avoid the outdoor settings. This is reflected in the increased respiratory and pulmonary diseases amongst people, especially children. 

*Second*, living in these communities (even for the very few who do) does not come cheap. It is a tax on your income to shut out “true” India from your living room. The disgruntlement of the upper middle class over high personal income tax rates could be seen as one manifestation of this dissatisfaction. This, in part, lead to the lowering of personal income tax rates in the latest budget for very rich people (by percentile-rank in India’s population), defying standard economic logic. 

*Third*, many more Indians are travelling abroad, especially to south-east Asian countries like Vietnam and Thailand. Many are ditching similar Indian tourist destinations like Goa. People are getting much more value for their rupee in terms of service, cleanliness,  and facilities. This, in turn, is working as a standard setting function for the Indian city people, who are coming back and voicing their discontent with what Indian cities have to offer.

*Fourth*, as the external environment for emigration remains difficult, we are likely to see an increase in “voice” of the citizens, as the situation grows worse. Fifth, it is also much less costly to organise today than before. The [radically networked societies](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/618a55c4cb03246776b68559/t/63231b184a7d447441c81727/1663245081843/Individual-Liberty-vs-Public-Security-in-a-RNS1.pdf#:~:text=The%20debate%20of%20liberty%20versus%20security%20gains,ability%20to%20evade%20conventional%20national%20security%20measures.) that we live in makes it much easier to mobilise large number of people using the power of the internet and much more difficult for hierarchical states to anticipate and curtail quickly. While there is hope, one has to wait and see how bad things get before they possibly start to get better.
